# Recurring AI Language Structures

These are detection patterns, not sentence templates to copy and not absolute
bans. A structure can be natural once; repetition, symmetry, unsupported
certainty, and interchangeable wording make it read as generated.

## Binary Reframe

Common forms:

- `X isn't Y. It's Z.`
- `The problem isn't X. The problem is Y.`
- `You don't need X. You need Y.`
- `This isn't about X. It's about Y.`
- `Not because X, but because Y.`

The recognizable mechanism is a denied contradiction followed by a categorical
closure. Preserve the underlying claim, but do not automatically package it as
two clipped sentences or inflate an interpretation into a fact.

## Claim-Correction Reveal

Common forms:

- `X said or promised A. It had or delivered B.`
- `The interface showed A. The reality was B.`
- `[Claim with a number]. [Clipped correction with a different number].`

The mechanism turns a discrepancy into a manufactured reveal: the first
sentence sets up a claim and the second acts as a numeric punchline. The facts
may be correct, but the clipped reversal still reads like model-generated
engagement copy.

Do not use this frame in the author's publishable prose. State the relationship in
ordinary syntax and include the relevant context, such as how the discrepancy
was found or why the two measurements can be compared. Use verified source material to explain the discrepancy without inventing an example.

Do not repair the pattern by joining the same two clauses with a dash or a
semicolon. Change the sentence relationship, not only the punctuation.

## Other Recurring Frames

- `Most people think X. The best people know Y.`
- `Everyone is focused on X while Y is what actually matters.`
- `The uncomfortable truth is...`
- `Let that sink in.`
- `Read that again.`
- `X changes everything.`
- `X sounds like Y. It isn't.`
- rhetorical question followed immediately by a neat answer;
- three clipped sentences with escalating intensity;
- three parallel examples followed by a universal lesson;
- personal anecdote followed by a generic motivational moral;
- ordinary observation presented as a revelation;
- repeated one-line paragraphs designed to simulate emphasis;
- a final sentence that restates the opening as a slogan.

## Planning and Discourse Signals

- **Fractal summaries:** previews and recaps at section and document level.
- **One-point dilution:** the same thought restated with new wording instead of
  developed or cut.
- **Listicle in prose:** each paragraph performs the next predictable item in
  an unstated numbered list.
- **Overly tidy anecdote:** every detail advances the thesis and nothing remains
  incidental, unresolved, or inconvenient.
- **Exhaustive coverage:** a short post tries to preserve every source point,
  so each sentence introduces a new argument or entity.
- **Uniform cadence:** sentences and paragraphs keep the same length, stress,
  and progression even when the ideas differ.
- **Signposted conclusion:** the ending summarizes the opening and adds generic
  uplift instead of stopping on the last concrete consequence.

Do not manufacture disorder to fix these signals. Cut, combine, or reorder
only when the thought becomes more faithful and natural.

## Sentence-Level Signals

- **Copula dodge:** `serves as`, `stands as`, `marks`, `represents`, or
  `functions as` where `is` or `has` says the same thing.
- **Participial tail:** a clause ending in `highlighting`, `underscoring`, or
  `showcasing` that adds an unsupported lesson.
- **False suspense:** `here's the kicker`, `this is where it gets interesting`,
  or a question whose answer is merely staged.
- **False range:** `from X to Y` when the named items do not form a meaningful
  range.
- **Analogy reflex:** an analogy added automatically even though the literal
  explanation is clearer.
- **Invented concept label:** a coined phrase presented as if it were already an
  established framework.

## Evidence and Stance Signals

- **Vague authority:** `studies show`, `experts say`, or `people are saying`
  without a named source or owned opinion.
- **Hedge stack:** several qualifications that avoid making the actual claim.
- **Automatic both-sidesing:** every position is balanced by a counterpoint
  regardless of relevance.
- **Uniform positivity:** every event becomes progress, a lesson, or an
  opportunity.
- **Significance marker:** a sentence exists only to announce what the previous
  fact means, such as `that's the point` or `that's the part that matters`.

Name the source, own the interpretation, preserve real uncertainty, and leave
something unresolved when the evidence is unresolved.

## False Humanization

Do not add any of the following unless they are present in the author's source
material and belong in the destination:

- typos, missing apostrophes, forced lowercase, or random slang;
- invented self-corrections, interruptions, complaints, or contradictions;
- doubled punctuation or fragments inserted only to appear spontaneous;
- irrelevant-but-plausible names, dates, prices, places, or tangents;
- deliberate inconsistency intended to alter a detector score.

Authenticity comes from authorship, evidence, selection, and point of view, not
from a checklist of human-looking defects.

## Review Method

1. Identify the factual claim beneath the frame.
2. Identify which wording or thought came from the author and preserve it when it
   still works.
3. Remove planning scaffolds, repeated points, and generic significance
   markers.
4. Write the relationship in ordinary syntax.
5. Restore contrast only if it reflects a real distinction.
6. Match certainty to evidence: observation, interpretation, hypothesis, or
   fact.
7. Compare nearby drafts so different topics do not reuse the same cadence.
8. When detector robustness matters, review the whole discourse shape without
   simulating mistakes or claiming a guaranteed pass.

Do not solve template repetition by swapping synonyms. Change the reasoning,
sentence relationship, evidence, or structure.
